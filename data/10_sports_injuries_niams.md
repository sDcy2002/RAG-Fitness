# Sports Injuries

## Overview
The term "sports injury" refers to injuries that happen during sports or exercise. They affect active people in general, not only athletes. This topic focuses on injuries of the musculoskeletal system: muscles, tendons, ligaments, bones, and other tissues.

Sports injuries fall into two categories:
- Acute injuries happen suddenly, for example from a fall or a twist. Examples are sprains and dislocations.
- Chronic injuries develop gradually from repetitive overload. Examples are tennis elbow and stress fractures.

Treatment depends on the type of injury. Many injuries respond to home care using the R-I-C-E method: Rest, Ice, Compression, and Elevation. Severe injuries may need professional care, such as physical therapy, a cast, a brace, or surgery. Rehabilitation is usually recommended before returning to the activity that caused the injury.

## Who gets sports injuries? (Risk factors)
- Incorrect exercise technique
- Overtraining
- Increasing intensity too quickly
- Playing the same sport year-round
- Running or jumping on hard surfaces
- Shoes that do not give enough support
- Not using proper protective equipment
- Previous injuries
- Certain anatomical features or poor flexibility
- Certain medicines, such as fluoroquinolone antibiotics, which are linked to tendinitis and tendon rupture

Risk also depends on the type of activity, age, and sex.

## Types of musculoskeletal injuries

### Bone fracture
A break in a bone, either from a sudden injury (acute fracture) or from repetitive stress (stress fracture).
- Acute fractures result from falls, collisions, or direct contact. Open fractures, where bone breaks through the skin, carry a risk of infection and need immediate care.
- Stress fractures occur mostly in the weight-bearing bones of the lower body (thigh bone, shin bones, and foot bones). They are common in gymnastics, tennis, basketball, and track. Running puts forces of two to three times a person's body weight on the lower limbs.
- Growth plate fractures affect the areas of cartilage near the ends of bones in growing children. Growth plates usually close by about age 18.

### Dislocation
The bones that form a joint are completely separated. Most dislocations come from contact sports and high-impact activities and need immediate treatment. They are most common in the shoulders, elbows, fingers, kneecaps, and knees.

### Sprain
A stretch or a partial or complete tear of a ligament, the band of connective tissue that stabilizes a joint. Sprains range from first degree (minimal stretching) to third degree (complete tear). The most vulnerable areas are the ankles, knees, and wrists.

### Strain
A twist, pull, or tear of a muscle, a tendon, or where they join. Strains can come from contact sports or repetitive motions such as tennis or golf, and range from a minor stretch to a complete tear.

### Tendinitis
Inflammation of a tendon, the flexible tissue that connects muscle to bone. It can affect the shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, or ankles, and comes from a sudden injury or repetitive motion. Tendons become less flexible with age.

### Bursitis
Inflammation of a bursa, a small fluid-filled sac that cushions bones and moving parts. Causes include a single injury, repetitive motion such as throwing, or prolonged pressure. It usually affects the shoulders, elbows, hips, or knees.

## Common sports injuries by body area

### Shoulder
- Rotator cuff injury: inflammation of the tendons or bursae of the four-muscle group that stabilizes the shoulder, from overuse or sudden injury. Common in swimmers, tennis players, and people who reach overhead often.
- Impingement: the shoulder blade presses on soft tissue underneath when the arm is lifted, which can lead to tendinitis and bursitis.
- Instability: the round end of the upper arm bone slips out of its shallow socket. Stretched or torn ligaments can lead to repeated dislocations.

### Elbow
- Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis): inflammation and small tears of tendons on the outer elbow, from racket sports or repetitive forearm use.
- Golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis): tendinitis causing pain on the inner elbow that may spread to the forearm and wrist.
- Little league elbow: a repetitive growth plate injury from throwing in young athletes, especially pitchers.
- Ulnar collateral ligament injury: repeated throwing tears the ligament on the inner elbow.

### Knee
- Runner's knee: pain or tenderness near or under the front of the kneecap. Affects runners, hikers, and cyclists.
- Fracture: kneecap fractures are the most common, usually from bad falls or direct blows.
- Dislocation: a strong impact pushes the kneecap out of its groove.
- Torn ligament: overextending or twisting tears knee ligaments. ACL injuries are especially common with sudden changes of direction or landing from a jump.
- Meniscal tear: a tear in the shock-absorbing cartilage from awkward twisting or pivoting.
- Tendon tear: more common in middle-aged people in running and jumping sports, often from forceful or awkward landings.

### Leg
- Groin pull: quick side-to-side movements strain the inner thigh muscles.
- Hamstring strain: tear of the three muscles at the back of the thigh from running, jumping, or sudden starts and stops.
- Shin splints: pain along the inner edge of the shinbone. Mainly affects runners, especially beginners.

### Ankle
- Ankle sprain: stretching or tearing of ligaments from rolling, twisting, or landing awkwardly. More common in pivoting sports such as volleyball and basketball.
- Achilles tendinitis: stretch, tear, or irritation of the tendon connecting the calf to the heel, the largest tendon in the body. Causes heel pain and stiffness, especially in the morning. Usually comes from overuse.

## Symptoms

Acute injury symptoms:
- Sudden, severe pain
- Extreme swelling or bruising
- Not being able to put weight on a leg, knee, ankle, or foot
- Not being able to move a joint normally
- Extreme weakness of a limb
- A bone or joint that is visibly out of place

Chronic injury symptoms:
- Pain during play or exercise
- Swelling and a dull ache at rest

## Causes
Acute injuries happen when the force of an impact is greater than the body part can withstand. Chronic injuries usually come from repeating the same motion over and over (repetitive overload). Overuse can weaken tissues and eventually lead to an acute injury.
